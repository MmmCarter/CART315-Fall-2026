My view of prototypes is probably the same as most people's - see them as unfinished versions of the final product. I thought the goal was to gradually add more features, improve the visuals, and make everything work properly. However, in “The Anatomy of Prototypes” by Lim, Stolterman, and Tenenberg, the authors argue that prototypes are not simply tools for testing ideas. They are also tools for exploring and developing them.

One concept I find particularly interesting is “Prototypes as Filters.” The author explains that a prototype doesn’t need to showcase every aspect of the design. Instead, it can focus on specific features while leaving other parts incomplete. When working on a game prototype, I sometimes spend too much time improving visual details or adding extra features before I fully understand whether the main mechanic is interesting. According to the article, a simpler prototype might actually be more useful because it allows me to focus on the question I am trying to answer.

I also found the idea of "prototypes as manifestations" meaningful. Creating a prototype isn’t just about transforming an idea that already exists in your mind into a physical or digital form; the process itself can also alter the original idea. I’ve had similar experiences while developing interactive projects. Sometimes a mechanic seems interesting in my head, but once I implement it and actually play with it, I discover problems or possibilities I had not considered. These unexpected discoveries often become an important part of the design process.

This article also got me thinking about how a prototype’s appearance and level of completion might influence people’s feedback. A polished prototype might make people assume that the design is already finished, while a rough one may encourage more suggestions and experimentation. But overall, I now understand more that prototyping isn’t about making the product as complete as possible, but rather about learning through the process of creation.
