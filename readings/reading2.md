One of the ideas in this article that resonated most with me was definitely Schön’s concept of "reflection in action". I generally thought of reflection as something that took place only after a project was completed. But Schön describes a different process: reflection can actually occur while we’re creating, especially when the results surprise us. Rather than simply viewing surprises as mistakes, we can treat them as insights and let them influence our next decisions. 

I can relate this idea strongly to my own experience using creative technology to produce work. When I use Unity, Blender, or audio editing software, I usually start with a fairly clear idea. However, the final piece rarely turns out exactly as originally planned. For example, when I adjust a parameter in a game, the change might be technically successful, but it can also create another unforeseen problem. then test it, observe what happens, and adjust the design again. In this sense, I am not simply controlling the software. The results produced by the software are also influencing my decisions. 

This makes it easier for me to understand what Schön means by "conversation with materials". He explains that designers rarely have a complete design in their heads and simply translate it into reality. Instead, materials give designers a kind of “backtalk” through unexpected results. I find this concept very helpful because it has changed the way I understand “failure.” An unexpected result doesn’t necessarily mean something went wrong; sometimes, it can actually lead me to discover a possibility I hadn’t even considered before experimenting.

The Growltiger example are also interesting. The software was originally designed as a design tool rather than a teaching tool, yet students learned through experimenting with structures and being surprised by the results. Which means that designers may not fully understand what they have created until real users begin to interact with it.

After reading this article, I feel that I can be more mindful of unexpected outcomes that arise during the creative process. When a result differs from my initial idea, I don’t necessarily need to “correct” it right away; instead, I can consider whether this unexpected outcome might yield better results and a more positive response than I originally anticipated.
